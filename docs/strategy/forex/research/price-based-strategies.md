## 1. Time-series momentum (TSMOM) and trend following in FX

### Takeaway
Trend following is the only price-based FX family with century-long, multi-author evidence that survives costs. But FX is one of its weaker sleeves. In 1985–2012 the reported gross Sharpe was about 0.87 for a diversified FX trend portfolio, against about 1.8 for all asset classes combined. A walk-forward test on seven majors over 2003–2025 got only about 0.43. Short-term (days-to-weeks) trend has stopped working since about 2009 in "small-tick" markets, which reportedly include currencies. Trend did well in the 2022 dollar and yen moves, had its second-worst drawdown on record in 2024–25, and recovered strongly in 2026. For a small bot, a daily-bar, multi-lookback (1–12 month), volatility-scaled ensemble on G10 pairs is codeable and defensible. A realistic expectation is a net Sharpe of about 0.2–0.5, mainly as a diversifier, not a stand-alone earner.

### Cited Findings

**Core academic evidence (all gross unless stated)**
- Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (JFE 2012) study 58 liquid futures and forwards (equity index, currency, commodity, bond). Returns persist for 1 to 12 months and then partly reverse over longer horizons. A diversified TSMOM portfolio earns abnormal returns with little exposure to standard factors and does best in extreme markets — [SSRN abstract](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2089463)
- MOP sample: January 1985 to December 2009. The diversified portfolio across all assets yields "a Sharpe ratio greater than one on an annual basis". The effect is robust across horizons and asset classes, "particularly when the look-back and holding periods are 12 months or less". Their Figure 2 plots the gross Sharpe of the 12-month rule for each instrument, including currency forwards, but the per-currency values were not readable in the extracts — [MOP paper, NYU Stern](https://w4.stern.nyu.edu/facdir/lpederse/papers/TimeSeriesMomentum.pdf); [Yale-hosted copy](https://fairmodel.econ.yale.edu/ec439/jpde.pdf)
- Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen, "Demystifying Managed Futures" (2013): 1-, 3- and 12-month lookbacks on 24 commodities, 9 equity futures, 13 bond futures and **12 currency forwards**, January 1985 to June 2012. The average **single-instrument gross Sharpe** was 0.29 (1-month), 0.36 (3-month) and 0.38 (12-month), so longer lookbacks did better on average. Asset-class portfolios diversified across the lookbacks ranged from 0.78 (equities) to 1.05 (commodities) — [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/intrinsic-momentum-diversified-across-futures/); [AQR page](https://www.aqr.com/library/journal-articles/demystifying-managed-futures)
- An AQR table surfaced by search gives gross Sharpe ratios of **Currencies 0.87**, Commodities 1.05, Equities 0.78, Fixed income 1.00 and **Diversified 1.79**. Its equity and commodity values match the Demystifying 1985–2012 asset-class range above, so this is very likely the same exhibit, but the extract did not show the sample period. A CXO summary gives the diversified figure as 1.8, while another summary gives about 1.3 for the 1-month signal diversified across all assets. These are likely different rows, but this is unverified — [AQR "Demystifying Managed Futures" PDF](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/Journal-Article/Demystifying-Managed-Futures.pdf); [CXO](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=24372)
- Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen, "A Century of Evidence on Trend-Following Investing" (JPM, Fall 2017): **67 markets including 12 currency pairs**, January 1880 to December 2016, monthly returns, positions scaled to a **10% annualised ex-ante portfolio volatility target**. Results are shown after estimated trading costs and **2% management plus 20% performance fees** — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing?aqrPDF=1); [PDF copy](https://oxfordstrat.com/coasdfASD32/uploads/2016/03/A-Century-of-Evidence-on-Trend-Following-Investing.pdf)
- Century paper, per CXO: **net returns were positive in every decade since 1880**, and so were the separate 1-, 3- and 12-month lookback portfolios. CXO reports a "0.4 average Sharpe ratio after 2%+20% fees". Gross Sharpe ratios of individual assets range from below 0.1 to above 1.0. (CXO's wording leaves it unclear whether 0.4 is a per-asset average or the portfolio figure, so check before quoting) — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/trend-following-with-intrinsic-momentum-over-the-very-long-run)
- Babu, Levine, Ooi, Pedersen & Stamelos, "Trends Everywhere" (AQR, JOIM): TSMOM on 82 previously unstudied instruments, **including emerging-market currencies**, plus 16 equity factors. The 12-month rule's gross Sharpe is **1.17 for traditional assets, 1.34 for "alternative" assets** (the group that includes EM FX), and 1.60 when the groups are combined. Returns continue for about 12 months and then tend to reverse (the reversal is mostly insignificant). No FX-only figure was in the extracts — [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/journal-article/trends-everywhere); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/time-series-momentum-works-everywhere/)
- Baltas & Kosowski, "Demystifying Time-Series Momentum Strategies". They (i) replace the ±1 sign of the 12-month return with a **continuous trend-strength signal in [−1, +1]**, and (ii) replace close-to-close volatility with the range-based **Yang–Zhang estimator**. Together these cut portfolio **turnover by more than a third with no statistically significant performance penalty**. (iii) A dynamic leverage adjustment using pairwise signed correlations beats the naive version, with a larger gap **after 2008**. Results hold **after costs**, modelled separately as roll and rebalancing costs. The sample is stated as 1984–2013 in the SSRN/CME version and 1974–2013 in the Imperial record (a conflict) — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2140091); [CME-hosted PDF](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/files/demystifiing-time-series-momentum-strategies.pdf); [Imperial](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/handle/10044/1/41472)
- Sepp & Lucic (arXiv, 2026) give a unified framework ("European", "American" and TSMOM-type systems). They derive an exact link between P&L, autocorrelation and drift of volatility-normalised returns, and show trend systems can profit when **long-horizon autocorrelation is positive even with short-term mean reversion**. They release a Python package (`trendfollowing`) that reproduces their exhibits on liquid futures — [arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.19497); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2607.19497.html)

**Post-2008 decay of short-term trend (directly relevant to timeframe choice)**
- Kurth, Eisler, Rej & Bouchaud (arXiv, July 2026) use about 100 liquid futures over 1995–2025. Trend has paid "for at least two centuries", but "since approximately 2009, **short-term trends have ceased to deliver reliable returns**". The deciding variable is **volatility-normalised tick size**: short-term trend P&L collapsed on small-tick contracts and stayed roughly intact on large-tick ones. Capacity, electronification and crowding explanations are rejected. The proposed mechanism is HFT market makers pulling liquidity in front of predictable directional flow — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01550); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/is-trend-still-your-friend-a-microstructural-account-of-the-demise-of-short-term-trend-following/)
- A third-party explainer of the same paper says it classifies **currency pairs (and equity indices) as small-tick** markets, and bonds and some commodities as large-tick. This comes from a secondary summary, not the full text — [alphaxiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2607.01550)

**Out-of-sample FX-only test (non-peer-reviewed)**
- A QuantInsti EPAT project tested TSMOM, MA crossover and channel breakout on **seven major pairs, 2003–2025**, with **23 rolling walk-forward windows (3-year train, 1-year test)** and parameters chosen for stability, not peak Sharpe. Only **USDJPY (0.78) and EURUSD (0.54)** cleared a 0.5 Sharpe bar, both with TSMOM. The best equal-weight multi-pair blend had a **Sharpe of 0.43 and CAGR of 1.24%**. The strategies averaged +6.20% in the 2008, 2020 and 2022 crisis windows. **Leverage made results worse** once financing costs and compounding were modelled. The extracts did not state the spread or commission assumptions — [QuantInsti](https://www.quantinsti.com/articles/trend-following-strategies-major-currency-markets-epat-project/)
- The same source notes that futures returns embed roll yield and term premia "simply absent in spot FX". A spot-price-only backtest therefore differs from the forward- and futures-based academic results — [QuantInsti](https://www.quantinsti.com/articles/trend-following-strategies-major-currency-markets/)

**Volatility targeting**
- Harvey, Hoyle, Korgaonkar, Rattray, Sargaison & Van Hemert, "The Impact of Volatility Targeting" (JPM 2018), cover over 60 assets with daily data from as early as 1926 to 2017. Vol targeting raises the Sharpe ratio for risk assets (e.g. US equities from 0.40 to 0.48–0.51), but **for bonds, currencies and commodities the Sharpe impact is negligible**. It does **reduce the likelihood of extreme returns across all asset classes, including currencies** — [Quantpedia (abstract)](https://quantpedia.com/the-impact-of-volatility-targeting-on-equities-bonds-commodities-and-currencies); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/volatility-targeting-improves-risk-adjusted-returns/)

**Industry index performance (net of fees; CTA indices are multi-asset, not FX-only)**
- **2022:** the SG Trend Index rose **27.35%** (its best year) and the SG CTA Index **20.1%** (best since 2000). Fixed income, **currencies** and energy gave the bulk of positive returns, with gains from falling bonds and **rising US dollar** trends — [Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/flat-december-sees-ctas-complete-record-year-2022/); [Alpha-Week](https://www.alpha-week.com/2022-cta-index-performance-review)

- **2024–25:** the SG Trend Index fell **20.4% from May 2024 to May 2025**, its **second-largest drawdown since 2000**, which Cambridge Associates links to tariff-driven whipsaws around April 2025 — [Cambridge Associates](https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/). ### Inferences
- **Lookbacks:** the evidence supports **1–12 month** lookbacks, with longer ones better on average (0.38 vs 0.29 single-asset gross Sharpe) and reversal beyond about 12 months. Combined with the post-2009 collapse of short-term trend in small-tick markets (which reportedly include FX), this argues against anything faster than about 1 month. A **1/3/12-month ensemble** (Hurst–Ooi–Pedersen) is the least-overfit default because every leg was positive in every decade since 1880.
- **Pairs:** use G10 USD majors (EURUSD, USDJPY, GBPUSD, AUDUSD, USDCAD, USDCHF, NZDUSD) and possibly liquid crosses (EURJPY, EURGBP). EM currencies helped in "Trends Everywhere", but retail spreads, financing and gap risk there are much worse. On a 7–10 pair FX-only book, correlation between USD legs is high, so effective diversification is perhaps 3–5 independent bets. That is why FX-only Sharpe is far below the multi-asset 1.8.
- **Codeable spec (literature parameters, no tuning):** on each daily close (taken at a fixed time *after* the 5pm New York rollover; see section 5):
  1. For each pair, compute the excess-return sign (or a Baltas–Kosowski-style continuous score clipped to [−1, +1]) over 21, 63 and 252 trading days, and average them into a signal in [−1, +1].
  2. Estimate ex-ante volatility with EWMA or Yang–Zhang on daily OHLC.
  3. Size each pair to an equal risk share of a portfolio target (the century paper uses 10% annualised).
  4. Rebalance weekly, or only when the target changes by more than about 10–20% (a buffer).
  5. Go long and short symmetrically. FX has no structural long bias, unlike the BTC bot's long/flat design.
- **Carry is embedded in the academic numbers:** academic TSMOM uses forwards or futures, whose returns include the interest differential. A spot backtest must add broker swap or rollover financing. Otherwise it overstates or understates returns depending on whether the trend is with or against carry (e.g. short JPY earned positive carry in 2022). The costs researcher's swap-markup data should feed into this.
- **Cost sensitivity at retail spreads (my arithmetic):** a 1–12 month ensemble typically trades about 5–20 round trips per pair per year. At 0.1–1.5 pips (about 0.1–1.4 bp), spread drag is about **0.01–0.3% a year per unit of notional**. Even at 5 bp all-in (wider crosses plus commission), 20 round trips cost about 1%. Against a plausible gross return of about 2–5% a year at a 10% vol target, costs matter but do not kill the strategy on majors. **Financing markups on held positions are likely the bigger retail drag** (see the QuantInsti leverage finding).
- **Vol targeting:** in FX it will **not raise Sharpe** much (Harvey et al.). Use it for **risk control and equal risk across pairs** (USDJPY and AUDUSD vol differ a lot), and to cut tail outcomes. Keep it, but do not expect alpha from it.
- **Time variation:** 1985–2012 is the most favourable FX trend period in the cited data (gross 0.87). The 2010s were weak for short-term FX trend (Kurth et al.). 2022 was a strong FX trend year (USD up, JPY down). 2024–25 was a severe multi-asset drawdown. 2026 has been strong. **A single-sleeve FX trend book should expect multi-year flat periods.**
- **Verdict:** **Build-worthy as the FX core**, but only as daily-bar, 1–12 month, vol-scaled, symmetric long/short on G10. Expect a net Sharpe of about 0.2–0.5 on an FX-only book (QuantInsti 0.43 out of sample; academic 0.87 gross in a favourable period; post-publication decay). Its best use is diversifying the BTC trend sleeve (the correlation is unknown; see Gaps).

### Gaps
- The **FX-only Sharpe from MOP's Table/Figure 2** and the **FX sleeve's net figure in the century paper** could not be read (no full-text access). The 0.87 figure is inferred to be Demystifying 1985–2012 gross.
- MOP's exact volatility-scaling parameters (commonly cited as a 40% per-instrument annualised vol target with an EWMA of about 60-day centre of mass) were **not verified** this session. Check the paper's Section 2 before coding them as "the MOP rule".

Neither SG Trend's 2024 full-year return nor its FX sub-index was found.
- No source measured the **correlation between FX trend and BTC trend** returns. That correlation determines whether FX trend is worth adding to the existing bot.
- No source documented Baltas–Kosowski results specifically for the FX sleeve.

## 2. Cross-sectional currency momentum (Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf 2012)

### Takeaway
Ranking currencies on past returns and going long winners, short losers produced up to about 10% a year gross in 1976–2010. But about a third was lost to bid-ask costs, and the profits came disproportionately from minor and EM currencies with high costs, high idiosyncratic volatility and capital-control or political risk. Post-2010 evidence (weaker sources) finds no significant returns. With 10–30 pairs needed, EM-heavy exposure and retail EM spreads, this is **not a candidate** for a small retail bot.

### Cited Findings
- Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf, "Currency Momentum Strategies" (JFE 106(3), 2012, pp. 660–684; BIS WP 366, 2011). Using up to 48 currencies over January 1976 to January 2010, there is a "significant cross-sectional spread in excess returns of up to 10% p.a. between past winner and loser currencies". It is not explained by standard risk factors and is "partially explained by transaction costs". It behaves differently from carry and is not highly correlated with benchmark technical rules. "Very effective **limits to arbitrage**... prevent momentum returns from being easily exploitable" — [BIS WP 366 (abstract)](https://www.bis.org/publ/work366.pdf); [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/eeejfinec/v_3a106_3ay_3a2012_3ai_3a3_3ap_3a660-684.htm); [City Research Online](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/3296)
- Net returns in the paper are defined as returns after bid-ask spreads, with spreads available for the full 1976–2010 period. A secondary citation reports the **gross** annualised mean excess return for the **1-month formation period as 10.18%**. A developed-country subsample (15 countries) is reported to show **very low profitability** (also secondary) — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=1809776); [City PDF](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/3296/1/Currency%20Momentum%20Strategies.pdf)
- Press summaries of the study say most momentum profits came from **less-liquid EM currencies (BRL, ZAR and PHP are named) rather than USD, EUR or GBP**. Winners and losers were skewed towards minor currencies with relatively high transaction costs, which accounted for **30–50% of momentum returns**, and "about a third of returns are lost in costs" — [Cass Knowledge](https://www.cass.city.ac.uk/faculties-and-research/research/cass-knowledge/inbusiness/2013/swept-along-by-currencies); [Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/currency-momentum-strategies-yield-striking-annual-returns-10-cent/)
- Time variation from a secondary account: momentum did badly in 2008, well in 2009, and in 2010 **poorly in G10 but well in EM** — [Euromoney](https://www.euromoney.com/article/b12kjlh23tzyry/momentum-trading-study-backs-case-for-fx-as-an-alpha-generating-asset-class)
- Filippou, Gozluklu & Taylor (JFQA 2018) find that global political risk is priced in the cross-section of currency momentum, robust to transaction costs and limits to arbitrage. Momentum winners carry exposure to (mostly US) political shocks, so the premium looks partly like **risk compensation** — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v53y2018i05p2227-2259_00.html); [Warwick WRAP](https://wrap.warwick.ac.uk/96779/)
- Burnside, Eichenbaum & Rebelo: in a 20-currency sample, equal-weighted momentum (like carry) gains roughly double the Sharpe from diversification across currencies, and carry and momentum payoffs are roughly uncorrelated. Separately, bid-ask spreads that **rise with order size** plus price pressure mean "the marginal Sharpe ratio can be zero even when the average Sharpe ratio is positive" — [NBER Reporter 2012](https://www.nber.org/reporter/2012number3/understanding-profitability-currency-trading-strategies); [NBER w16942](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16942.pdf)
- EM-only evidence (Tajaddini, Otago PhD 2013): long-short EM currency momentum earned about **1–3% a year after actual transaction costs**, more volatile than majors. The related journal version found **profits declined over time, with most strategies losing money after costs in the last five years of the sample** — [Otago thesis](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/doctoral/Momentum-Trading-Strategies-in-Financial-Markets/9926480445101891); [Otago journal article record](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Do-momentum-based-trading-strategies-work-in/9926517113901891)
- Post-2010: a 2025 Aalto University **bachelor's thesis** (32 currencies, January 2010 to January 2025) finds **no statistically significant excess returns for any of 25 momentum portfolios** after costs. This is a weak source but the only post-2010 test found — [Aalto](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/0db57fa9-e5c6-4b7d-9dd8-1ae15a8940c6)
- Weekly-horizon evidence (Raza, Massey thesis, 63 currencies): weekly currency returns show **cross-sectional momentum, not reversal**, with break-even transaction costs of **2 to 97 bp** depending on the portfolio — [Massey](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/6d3b0d99-f9ec-419e-b10e-b3902216979c/download)
- Krohn, Mueller & Whelan's earlier draft ("FX Premia Around the Clock") finds that **currency momentum returns are earned mostly overnight**, while carry is earned intraday (US hours) — [AUT-hosted draft, 2018](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/190753/Krohn_Mueller_Whelan_Jun2018_FXPremiaAroundTheClock.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Codeable rule (for reference):** each month, rank N currencies vs USD on their past 1–12 month excess return (spot change plus forward discount), go long the top tercile or quintile and short the bottom, equal-weight, hold 1 month. With only G10, that is 9 currencies and terciles of 3. The academic edge sits mostly outside G10.
- **Cost sensitivity:** the original edge was cut by about a third at **institutional** bid-ask spreads. The currencies carrying the returns (BRL, ZAR, PHP and similar) are exactly those where retail spreads, swap markups and availability are worst, and some are non-deliverable. Burnside et al.'s size-dependent spreads matter less at $1k–$50k, but retail spread markups more than offset that.
- **Overlap:** a G10-only cross-sectional book is close to a dollar-neutral version of TSMOM, and the academic papers find developed-country momentum weak. It adds little to section 1.
- **Verdict:** **Do not build.** It is EM-concentrated, cost-fragile, apparently gone after 2010 (weak evidence), needs many simultaneous positions, and risks breaking the repo's single-instrument architecture.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed post-2010 replication of G10 or EM cross-sectional currency momentum was found. The developed-only net return table, turnover and drawdown figures from MSSS were not retrievable.
- No 2022–2026 currency-momentum factor returns were found (searches returned only equity momentum indices).

## 3. Classic technical rules (MA crossovers, filter rules, channel/Donchian breakouts) under data-snooping controls

### Takeaway
Simple filter and moving-average rules on dollar exchange rates earned genuine risk-adjusted profits in the 1970s–80s, and those profits had **disappeared for major pairs by the early 1990s**. More complex or less-studied rules decayed more slowly. Studies with formal multiple-testing corrections split. Hsu–Taylor–Wang (2016, 21,000+ rules, 30 currencies) still find excess profits, especially in EM. Kuang–Schröder–Wang (2014, about 26,000 rules on 10 EM currencies) find "almost all" profits vanish after data-snooping correction, and Zarrabi–Snaith–Coakley (2017) find profits only "sometimes". Real retail account data show technical-indicator use is associated with **worse** returns. The edge did not "move to EM" in a way a small retail bot can harvest.

### Cited Findings
- Neely, Weller & Ulrich, "The Adaptive Markets Hypothesis: Evidence from the FX Market" (JFQA 44(2), 2009) run true out-of-sample tests of rules from earlier studies. The 1970s–80s excess returns "were genuine and not just the result of data mining", but "these profit opportunities had **disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving average rules**". Returns to less-studied rules (channel, ARIMA, genetic programming, Markov) also declined but "**have probably not completely disappeared**". They conclude this fits the AMH, not the EMH — [St. Louis Fed WP 2006-046 (EconPapers)](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/fipfedlwp/2006-046.htm); [Cambridge/JFQA](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/9D336CDCA83233819EB5CDD0F4BC0DAA)
- Neely & Weller, "Lessons from the Evolution of Foreign Exchange Trading Strategies" (St. Louis Fed WP 2011-021) model a trader choosing among FX technical rules, carry and US equities. Trading-rule returns "still exist in forex markets — with substantial stability in the types of rules — though they have **migrated to emerging markets** to a considerable degree" — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/fipfedlwp/2011-021.htm)
- Neely & Weller, "Technical Analysis in the Foreign Exchange Market" (survey, St. Louis Fed WP 2011-001 / Wiley Handbook of Exchange Rates): simple rules on dollar rates gave "15 years of positive, risk-adjusted returns during the 1970s and 80s before those returns were extinguished". More complex rules have since found "more modest returns". The survey summary also reports that, in Neely & Weller's **intraday** study, "when realistic transaction costs and trading hours are taken into account, they find no evidence of excess returns" (the attribution to a specific intraday paper is from the search summary) — [FRASER PDF](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/docs/publications/frbsl_wp/2011-001.pdf)
- Olson (2004), "Have trading rule profits in the currency markets declined over time?" (J. Banking & Finance), is cited by later reviews as part of the evidence that FX trading-rule profits **declined out of sample**. Its own numbers were not retrievable — [Park & Irwin working paper citing it](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/37487/files/AgMAS04_04.pdf); [ISC record](https://search.isc.ac/Inventory/10/193559.htm)
- Qi & Wu (2006), "Technical Trading-Rule Profitability, Data Snooping, and Reality Check: Evidence from the FX Market" (JMCB 38(8), pp. 2135–2158), test 2,127 rules. The best are significantly profitable. White's Reality Check "does not change the basic conclusions for the full sample", but data snooping "is more serious in the second half of the sample", where **profitability becomes much weaker**, read as growing market efficiency. Cross rates confirm the results — [Project MUSE abstract](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/209043/summary)
- Hsu, Taylor & Wang (2016), "Technical trading: Is it still beating the foreign exchange market?" (J. International Economics 102, pp. 188–208), use about 45 years of daily data on **30 developed and EM currencies** and **over 21,000 rules** (filter, MA, support-resistance, channel breakout, oscillator). Using a stepwise SPA test against data snooping, they find "predictability and excess profitability in both developed and emerging currencies", with sub-period and regional variation attributed to "temporarily not-fully-rational behavior and market immaturity" — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2765673); [WUSTL profile](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/technical-trading-is-it-still-beating-the-foreign-exchange-market/)
- HTW cost model (working paper): average **one-way costs just under 4.5 bp for developed and just under 21 bp for EM currencies**. A later citing paper reports 2 bp and 6 bp instead (a version or definition conflict). CXO's reading is that effectiveness "**diminishes over time**" but "may still be attractive for emerging market currencies", and that the computational burden of searching for the best rules is "likely beyond the reach of most investors" — [technicalanalyst-hosted SSRN PDF](https://technicalanalyst-cdn-1.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/13144511/SSRN-id2765673.pdf); [CXO Advisory](https://cxoadvisory.com/technical-trading/updated-comprehensive-long-term-test-of-technical-currency-trading)
- By contrast, Hsu & Taylor (2013) and Coakley et al. (2016) found "little or no evidence" of technical-rule profitability after costs and data snooping (as characterised in the search summary of HTW's context). Coakley, Marzano & Nankervis (IRFA 45, 2016) cover **22 USD-quoted currencies over 1996–2015** — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finana/v45y2016icp273-282.html); [St Andrews CDMA WP](https://www.st-andrews.ac.uk/~wwwecon/repecfiles/2/1302.pdf)
- Zarrabi, Snaith & Coakley, "FX technical trading rules can be profitable sometimes!" (IRFA 49, 2017, pp. 113–127), test **7,650 rules on six USD currencies, 1994–2014**, with the Barras–Scaillet–Wermers false discovery rate and transaction costs. Out of sample, many rules are profitable over **short windows** on a Sharpe basis, but "they are **not consistently profitable**", consistent with the AMH — [Essex repository](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18805/)
- Kuang, Schröder & Wang (2014), "Illusory profitability of technical analysis in emerging foreign exchange markets" (Int. J. Forecasting), test **25,988 strategies on 10 EM currencies**. The best showed **over 30% annual mean excess return**, and standard tests flag hundreds to thousands as significant, but "**almost all of these profits vanish once the data snooping bias is taken into account**... the profitability of technical analysis is illusory" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/bir/birmec/13-09.html); [Birmingham](https://research.birmingham.ac.uk/en/publications/illusory-profitability-of-technical-analysis-in-emerging-foreign-/)
- Park & Irwin's survey (J. Economic Surveys 21(4), 2007) finds technical analysis widely used in FX and futures, with about 30–40% of practitioners rating it important at horizons up to six months. Problems flagged: **data snooping, ex-post selection of rules and difficulty establishing transaction costs**. Some authors (Marsh 2000; LeBaron 2002; Olson 2004) found profitability **declined over time** — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jecsur/v21y2007i4p786-826.html); [AgEcon working paper](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/37487/files/AgMAS04_04.pdf)
- As cited by Abbey & Doukas, the Park & Irwin review found **24 of 38** FX studies reporting technical profitability, with profits of **5–10% a year**. These are rule-on-price backtests, not trader returns — [Abbey & Doukas PDF](https://www.efmaefm.org/0DOUKAS/publications/pdf/IsTechnicalAnalysisProfitable_12-14-2011.pdf)
- **Actual retail outcomes.** Abbey & Doukas (J. Portfolio Management 2012) use daily returns and transactions of **428 individual currency-trader accounts, 2005–2009**. Technical analysis is **negatively associated with performance**. Named indicators include RSI, Bollinger Bands, MACD and an 8/18-day MA crossover — [Abbey & Doukas PDF](https://www.efmaefm.org/0DOUKAS/publications/pdf/IsTechnicalAnalysisProfitable_12-14-2011.pdf); [ODU dissertation](https://digitalcommons.odu.edu/businessadministration_etds/6)
- Older baseline: Levich & Thomas (bootstrap, currency futures 1976–1990) found simple technical rules "very often led to profits that are highly unusual". This is the pre-1990s regime that later disappeared — [NBER w3818](https://www.nber.org/papers/w3818)

### Inferences
- **Rules worth coding only as members of the trend ensemble:** a dual MA crossover (e.g. 50/200-day), a Donchian/channel breakout (e.g. 20–120 day highs and lows, entry on the N-day high/low, exit on the N/2-day opposite), and a filter rule are all **trend detectors on the same 1–12 month horizon** as TSMOM. Their FX evidence is a subset of the trend evidence in section 1. They should be averaged inside that ensemble, not run as separate "strategies" with separately tuned parameters.
- **Why costs are not the main issue on majors:** HTW's developed-market cost assumption (about 4.5 bp one way) is several times **wider** than a 0.1–1.5 pip retail ECN spread on EURUSD (about 0.04–0.7 bp one way). The profits on majors died because the **gross** edge faded after the 1980s, not because of costs. In EM, where some papers still find profits, the cost assumption of about 21 bp one way is likely **below** what retail brokers charge on exotic pairs once financing is included. That is the wrong way round for a retail trader.
- **Data-mining scale:** these studies test 2,000–26,000 rules. Results flip between "still profitable" (HTW, stepwise SPA) and "illusory" (KSW, data-snooping correction) depending on the test and sample. Any retail grid search over MA lengths and filters on a few majors is effectively running a small version of the same experiment without the correction. **Use literature parameters, pre-register them, and do not tune.**
- **Verdict:** **Do not build as stand-alone strategies on majors.** At most, use MA or Donchian variants as extra lookback members of the trend ensemble. Do not chase EM technical profits at retail.

### Gaps
- Olson (2004) numbers (risk-adjusted MA profits by sub-period) and HTW's sub-period results by decade could not be retrieved. HTW reports "variation in sub-periods", and CXO says effectiveness declined, but the exact decade-by-decade Sharpe or return figures are unverified.
- Coakley–Marzano–Nankervis (2016) conclusions are known only through a secondary characterisation ("little or no evidence after costs").
- No data-snooping-corrected study covering **2015–2026** specifically was found.

## 4. Mean reversion: short-horizon reversal, RSI/Bollinger fades, Asian-range fading, cointegration on crosses, and the EURCHF lesson

### Takeaway
I found **no cost-surviving, out-of-sample evidence** for retail mean-reversion strategies on FX majors. Bollinger-band rules fail after costs, including an intraday EURUSD study over 2009–2016. Weekly currency returns show momentum rather than reversal. The documented reliable FX reversals happen around benchmark fixes at minute-to-hour scale, where institutional flow drives them. No peer-reviewed cost-inclusive evidence was found for AUDNZD, EURGBP or EURCHF pairs or cointegration trading. The EURCHF floor removal (15 January 2015, about a 30% franc jump, broker insolvencies) is the textbook case of a low-volatility mean-reversion strategy that is short a catastrophic tail.

### Cited Findings
- No daily-frequency FX short-term reversal study with cost-adjusted returns was found. At weekly horizons, Raza (Massey thesis, 63 currencies) finds **momentum, not reversal**, with break-even costs of 2–97 bp — [Massey](https://mro.massey.ac.nz/bitstreams/6d3b0d99-f9ec-419e-b10e-b3902216979c/download)
- Around the **London 4pm WM/Reuters fix**, Evans (J. Banking & Finance 87, 2018; 21 currencies over a decade) finds "extraordinary volatility and **negative serial correlation**" in price changes, i.e. short-lived reversals, which a competitive microstructure model did not predict — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v87y2018icp233-247.html); [MPRA](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/81583)
- Krohn, Mueller & Whelan (JF 2024) document pervasive, highly significant **return reversals around the Tokyo, Frankfurt/ECB and London fixes** for the top nine currencies over 21 years: the USD appreciates into fixes and depreciates afterwards — [Bank of Canada SWP 2021-48](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2021/10/staff-working-paper-2021-48/); [BIS slides](https://www.bis.org/events/221213_bis_bdi_ecb_exchange_rates/mueller.pdf)
- Bollinger Bands (Lento, Gradojevic & Wright 2007): "after adjusting for transaction costs, the BB are consistently unable to earn profits in excess of the buy-and-hold"; a contrarian version improved profitability. The search extract did not confirm which FX series or whether the tests were out of sample — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/raflxx/v3y2007i4p263-267.html)
- A Bollinger strategy on **intraday EURUSD tick data, 2009–2016**, "produced an overall **net loss** from trading" (Vaughters, Liberty University doctoral dissertation, 2018; not a top-journal source) — [Liberty University](https://digitalcommons.liberty.edu/doctoral/1864)
- Real accounts: RSI and Bollinger use is part of the indicator set **negatively associated** with individual FX trader performance in 2005–2009 (Abbey & Doukas, JPM 2012) — [Abbey & Doukas PDF](https://www.efmaefm.org/0DOUKAS/publications/pdf/IsTechnicalAnalysisProfitable_12-14-2011.pdf)
- Theory: trend-following P&L can be positive when long-horizon autocorrelation is positive **even if short-term mean reversion is present** (Sepp & Lucic 2026). Short-horizon reversal and long-horizon trend can coexist, which also means a mean-reversion overlay can fight the trend core — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/p/arx/papers/2607.19497.html)
- Pairs and cointegration: **no FX-cross-specific (AUDNZD, EURGBP, EURCHF) cost-inclusive study was found**. General evidence: a crypto pairs-trading thesis found cointegration pairs "mostly unprofitable under transaction costs" — [Charles University](https://dspace.cuni.cz/handle/20.500.11956/109634). A practitioner note warns that FX pairs with different quote currencies must be converted to a common quote currency before Johansen or CADF tests — [Quantra forum](https://quantra.quantinsti.com/community/t/mean-reversion-strategy-portfolio-return-of-currencies/22656) (forum, low quality)
- **EURCHF, 15 January 2015:** the SNB removed its 1.20 EUR/CHF floor without warning, and the franc jumped by a record of about 30%. Most retail clients were long EURCHF or USDCHF. **FXCM clients ran up about $225m of negative balances**, and FXCM needed about $300m of emergency financing from Leucadia. **Alpari UK became insolvent** (filing or administration dates differ across sources). IG took about a £30m hit, and Excel Markets and Global Brokers NZ ceased operations. The EU and UK later mandated **negative balance protection** for retail — [Finance Magnates](https://www.financemagnates.com/forex/brokers/fxcm-publishes-data-of-snb-mishandling-of-the-swiss-franc/); [Michelmores](https://www.michelmores.com/commercial-litigation-insight/swiss-franc-turbulence/); [Investing.com](https://investing.com/news/economy-news/swiss-franc-shock-shuts-some-fx-brokers;-regulators-investigate-323819); [EarnForex history](https://www.earnforex.com/guides/history-of-2015-eurchf-floor-failure/)

### Inferences
- **Why the EURCHF episode matters for strategy design, not only broker risk:** with a floor in place, EURCHF looked like the ideal mean-reversion pair: low realised volatility, bounded downside, high win rate. A "buy near the floor, fade deviations" rule would have shown an excellent backtest Sharpe right up to the day it lost a multiple of its cumulative profit, with stops gapped through. The general lessons:
  1. Any FX mean-reversion backtest on a pegged, floored, banded or heavily managed pair is implicitly **short a policy-change option**.
  2. Historical volatility-based stops and sizing do not protect against gaps.
  3. Avoid managed pairs (CHF in 2011–15, HKD, CNH, DKK, Gulf pegs and similar).

  The 2011 floor start date is from general knowledge, not verified this session.
- **Cross "pairs" like AUDNZD and EURGBP** are economically linked, but their spreads can trend for years with rate differentials (the carry researcher's domain). With no cost-inclusive evidence, they are hypotheses, not strategies.
- **Cost sensitivity:** mean-reversion fades on 1h–4h bars typically trade hundreds of round trips a year. At 1–1.5 bp per round trip on majors, 250 trades ≈ 2.5–3.75% a year of notional, and crosses cost more (see the break-even arithmetic in section 5). Gross edges in the cited studies were not shown to clear that.
- **Interaction with the trend core:** fading short-term moves partly cancels a trend book's entries. A combined system would need explicit netting and would raise turnover.
- **Verdict:** **Do not build.** At most, use a short-term overextension filter on the *timing* of trend entries (e.g. delay a trend entry after a 2-sigma one-day move), validated separately. It is not a source of returns.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed, cost-inclusive, out-of-sample study of **RSI(2)/RSI(14) fades on EURUSD**, **Asian-session range fading**, or **cointegration trading of AUDNZD, EURGBP or EURCHF** was found.
- Effect sizes (bp) of the fix-related reversals (Evans; Krohn et al.) were not in the extracts.

## 5. Intraday and session effects: time-of-day patterns, fixes, London/Asian breakouts, intraday momentum and the 5pm New York rollover

### Takeaway
Real, statistically strong intraday regularities exist in FX: currencies weaken in their home business hours (Ranaldo 2009; Breedon & Ranaldo 2013), and the dollar traces a W-shaped path around the Tokyo, ECB/Frankfurt and London fixes (Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, JF 2024). Month-end London-fix flows are partly predictable from equity hedging (Melvin & Prins 2015). But the authors themselves describe these effects as exploitable by investors with **below-average transaction costs** (market makers, large institutions). The only systematic retail-style London-breakout backtest I found lost 60%. For a retail bot these effects are best used as **execution-timing rules** (when to rebalance and when to avoid trading), not as stand-alone strategies.

### Cited Findings
- **Ranaldo (J. Banking & Finance 33(12), 2009), "Segmentation and time-of-day patterns in foreign exchange markets":** domestic currencies systematically **depreciate during domestic working hours and appreciate during foreign working hours**. The patterns are statistically and economically highly significant and robust to calendar effects across many years. The proposed mechanism is home-currency bias plus market segmentation, which creates cyclical dealer inventory imbalances — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v33y2009i12p2199-2206.html); [SNB WP 2007-03](https://www.snb.ch/fr/publications/research/working-papers/2007/working_paper_2007_03)
- **Breedon & Ranaldo, "Intraday Patterns in FX Returns and Order Flow" (JMCB 45(5), 2013, pp. 953–965):** with 10 years of high-frequency data, currencies tend to depreciate in local trading hours, and the pattern shows up in order flow too. "In the case of EUR/USD, it can form a **simple, profitable trading strategy**." An earlier draft gives the example that EURUSD tends to **fall in the European morning and rise in US hours** — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/mcb/jmoncb/v45y2013i5p953-965.html); [SNB WP 2011-04](https://www.snb.ch/en/publications/research/working-papers/2011/working_paper_2011_04); [AEA 2009 draft](https://swlb1.aeaweb.org/conference/2009/retrieve.php?pdfid=301)
- **Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, "Foreign Exchange Fixings and Returns around the Clock" (JF 2024; BoC SWP 2021-48):** the USD **appreciates in the run-up to FX fixes and depreciates afterwards**, tracing a **W-shaped** 24-hour pattern driven by the Tokyo fix (Asian hours) and the Frankfurt/ECB and London fixes. The reversals hold for the **top nine currencies over 21 years**, every weekday and month, and are "highly statistically significant", implying daily swings above **$1 billion**. Natural experiments show that the existence of the published fix sets the timing. The most likely driver is **dealer hedging** of an unconditional demand for USD at the fixes — [Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2021/10/staff-working-paper-2021-48/); [WU Vienna conference version](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2022/Paper/Session_III_1a_Krohn__foreign_Exchange_Fixings_Returns_Around_The_Clock_0712.pdf)
- From spring to autumn the pattern near the **Tokyo fix shifts by about one hour**, because Japan does not observe daylight saving time. This is a useful detail for coding session windows in UTC — [AEA 2020 preliminary](https://benny.aeaweb.org/conference/2020/preliminary/paper/2dedYfDH)
- Related BoC finding: order-flow imbalances from **large banks' trading desks** matter more for intraday drifts than flows from speculators or hedgers — [Bank of Canada SWP 2021-48](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/2021/10/staff-working-paper-2021-48/)
- **"FX Premia Around the Clock" (2018 draft, same authors):** a portfolio long all foreign currencies earns **under 1% a year close-to-close**, but splitting the day gives **almost +5% intraday and almost −4% overnight**. About 75% of carry (HML) and about 80% of dollar-carry returns are earned in the US trading day (a later AFA 2020 version says 100% of dollar carry is earned in **European** hours, so the versions conflict). Momentum is most profitable **overnight**. The authors say the effect "may be exploitable by investors that are able to benefit from **lower than average transaction costs**" — [AUT-hosted draft](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/190753/Krohn_Mueller_Whelan_Jun2018_FXPremiaAroundTheClock.pdf); [AEA 2019 draft](https://topcat.aeaweb.org/conference/2019/preliminary/paper/QQ73KSH8)
- **London 4pm fix and month-end hedging:** Melvin & Prins (J. Financial Markets 22, 2015) find that **equity-market appreciation over the month predicts currency depreciation before the month-end fix**, consistent with equity managers rebalancing FX hedges at the last fix of the month — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v22y2015icp50-72.html); [ECB workshop slides](https://ecb.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_Workshop_MELVIN_PRINS_Equity%20hedging%20and%20exchange%20rates%20Nov%202013.pdf). A Barclays month-end note says actual flows at the fix "routinely deviate" from such models, so the forecasts are "directional at best" — [Newsquawk/Barclays](https://www.newsquawk.com/headlines/barclays-month-end-moderate-usd-selling)
- The fix itself: Ito & Yamada describe the WM/Reuters 4pm price as computed from trades in a short window around 4:00pm (one minute in their pre-reform sample), and bank behaviour around it was the subject of regulatory investigations and fines — [Columbia WP 344](https://business.columbia.edu/sites/default/files-efs/imce-uploads/Research/Research_Papers/WP_344.Ito_.Yamada.Was_Forex_Fixing_Fixed.Final_.8-26-15%20(1).pdf); [Evans 2018](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbfina/v87y2018icp233-247.html)
- **Intraday momentum:** Elaut, Frömmel & Lampaert, "Intraday momentum in FX markets: Disentangling informed trading from liquidity provision" (J. Financial Markets 37, 2018, pp. 35–51). Literature reviews cite it as showing that the **first half-hour return predicts the last half-hour return** in an FX market. The abstract, currency, sample and trading results **could not be retrieved** (paywalled, and the fetch failed) — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/finmar/v37y2018icp35-51.html); [citing review, Reading](https://reading-9.eprints-hosting.org/95566/1/Accepted-Version.pdf)
- **London-open / Asian-range breakout:** **no academic evidence found**. A vendor backtest of GBPUSD, 2016–2026, on 15-minute bars (Asian range 00:00–06:00 UTC, break entry, 20-pip stop, 40-pip target) returned **−60%** (buy-and-hold +2.1%), with a profit factor of 0.72 and a 33.7% win rate. This is a commercial source with an unstated cost model — [Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/london-breakout-gbp-usd). Forum reports are anecdotal and regime-dependent: profitable in trends, poor in ranges — [BabyPips forum](https://forums.babypips.com/t/london-breakout-strategy-1h-timeframe-modified/326993)
- **5pm New York rollover:** in OTC spot the **value date rolls at 5:00pm NY**. Positions are carried to the next settlement date, financing is applied, banks close one book and open another, liquidity providers "scale back or step away", and it is "often the **least liquid hour** of the FX day". CME FX futures halt for 60 minutes (5–6pm NY) while most retail platforms keep quoting — [CME Group, 2026](https://www.cmegroup.com/articles/2026/when-fx-futures-pause.html)
- Retail pass-through: broker spreads are the best LP bid/ask plus markup, so LP widening at rollover is passed straight to clients. Estimates of how long it lasts conflict: about 5 minutes on a forum, versus 30–60 minutes before pricing normalises on a broker page. Wednesday rollover carries three days of financing (for the weekend) — [BlackBull](https://blackbull.com/en/education-hub/understanding-roll-over-and-widening-spreads-2); [BabyPips forum](https://forums.babypips.com/t/is-it-normal-or-not-for-spread-being-widen-like-that/46027); [bvtot thread](https://bvtot.com/threads/forex-spreads-why-do-they-peak-at-22-00-gmt.33477)

### Inferences
- **Break-even arithmetic for a once-a-day session trade (my calculation).** Assume EURUSD annual vol of 8%, so daily σ ≈ 50 bp, and all-in retail cost c per round trip. The gross annualised Sharpe needed **just to break even** is about (c / σ_hold) × √250, where σ_hold is the vol over the holding window:
  - Full-day hold (σ ≈ 50 bp), c = 1.5 bp: break-even Sharpe ≈ **0.47**.
  - 3-hour hold (σ ≈ 18 bp, assuming uniform intraday variance), c = 1.5 bp: ≈ **1.3**.
  - 1-hour hold (σ ≈ 10 bp): ≈ **2.3**.
  - At c = 5 bp (crosses, or majors at a poor broker), multiply these by about 3.3.

  Asian-session variance is below average, so Asian-range strategies are **worse** than the uniform-variance numbers suggest. This explains why the academic authors restrict exploitability to low-cost institutions.
- **Practical uses at retail (cheap and plausible, not proven):**
  1. **Never execute at or just after 5pm NY.** Take the daily signal on the NY close but execute 1–2 hours later, or in the London/NY overlap.
  2. **Avoid trading in the minutes around the 4pm London fix and the Tokyo fix** (volatility and reversals, per Evans and Krohn et al.).
  3. If a trend rebalance needs to **buy USD**, doing it *after* a fix rather than into it leans with the W-pattern (USD tends to weaken after fixes). The expected saving is a few bp per trade, which is valuable only because it costs nothing.
  4. Treat the Ranaldo/Breedon "home-hours depreciation" as a minor execution tilt (e.g. buy EUR in the European morning, sell it in US hours), not a strategy.
- **Fix-flow trading is not available at retail.** The fix price is not a price a retail client can trade at, and fix effects come from institutional benchmark orders.
- **Verdict:** **Do not build session or intraday strategies** (London breakout, Asian range fade, intraday momentum, fix trading). **Do** encode time-of-day rules in the execution layer of whatever strategy is built.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes in bp, and any **net-of-cost Sharpe**, for the Ranaldo, Breedon–Ranaldo and Krohn–Mueller–Whelan strategies were not in the extracts. Whether the EURUSD time-of-day strategy survives **2015–2026** spreads, after the 2013–2015 fix scandal and the reform, is unknown.
- Elaut et al. (2018) currency, sample, results and economic value could not be retrieved.
- The post-2015 WM fix window (widened after the FSB reform; commonly cited as 5 minutes) was not verified this session.
- No academic study of the London-open or Asian-range breakout was found. The single vendor backtest is weak evidence.
- No hard data was found on retail spread levels at 5pm NY by broker, which belongs to the broker researcher.

## 6. Machine learning and deep learning for FX forecasting

### Takeaway
Careful ML work on exchange rates finds that **regularised linear models** can beat the random walk at a monthly horizon, but only with economic predictors, and that deep nonlinear models add little (Filippou, Rapach, Taylor & Zhou, JFQA 2026). Deep-learning trend models (LSTM, Momentum Transformer) improve multi-asset futures trend before costs but are fragile to costs above about 2–3 bp. Most 2024–2026 EURUSD deep-learning papers report only forecast-error metrics, not net trading results. The few with costs use short (about 1-year) test windows or flag that complex models fail at realistic costs while simple ones degrade more gracefully. **No credible, multi-year, net-of-retail-cost ML FX strategy was found.**

### Cited Findings
- **Filippou, Rapach, Taylor & Zhou**, "Exchange Rate Prediction with Machine Learning and a Smart Carry Portfolio" (CEPR DP15305, 2020; JFQA version dated 2026). They use 70 predictors (country characteristics, global variables and their interactions) and an **elastic net** to forecast **monthly** FX changes out of sample. The forecast "consistently outperforms the naive no-change benchmark", improves carry especially during and after the GFC, and "**when the authors allow for more complex deep learning models, nonlinearities do not appear substantial**". They impose economic constraints to reduce overfitting. The most important interactions are country characteristics × FX volatility — [CEPR DP15305 PDF](https://repec.cepr.org/repec/cpr/ceprdp/DP15305.pdf); [JFQA PDF](https://jfqa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/26402_Econ_Fundamentals_Machine_Learning.pdf); [VSFX 2021 version](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2021/Papers/Rapach_SmartCarry_07-02-2021.pdf). (These are fundamentals-based, which overlaps the macro researcher's scope, but they are the most careful ML exchange-rate study found.)
- A 2025 Federal Reserve paper notes the **exchange-rate predictability puzzle persists**, underlining "the robustness of the random walk benchmark" — [Fed FEDS 2025-089](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2025089pap.pdf)
- **Lim, Zohren & Roberts, "Enhancing Time-Series Momentum Strategies Using Deep Neural Networks"** (J. Financial Data Science, Fall 2019). Deep nets learn trend and position size jointly, trained to maximise Sharpe, inside a vol-scaling framework, on **88 continuous futures**. The Sharpe-optimised LSTM beats traditional methods by **more than 2× without costs** and still outperforms **with costs up to about 2–3 bp**. A turnover regulariser is needed for less liquid assets — [arXiv 1904.04912](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.04912); [Quantpedia summary](https://quantpedia.com/using-deep-neural-networks-to-enhance-time-series-momentum/)
- **Momentum Transformer** (Wood, Giegerich, Roberts & Zohren): an attention-based architecture said to outperform benchmark TSMOM and mean-reversion strategies; details are paywalled — [Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/cutting-edge/7956074/trading-with-the-momentum-transformer-an-interpretable-deep-learning-architecture). An equities extension reports about 4.14% average return and a Sharpe of 1.12 — [arXiv 2412.12516](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.12516v1)
- **EXFormer** (Liu, Ślepaczuk & Tang; arXiv 2512.12727, December 2025, revised January 2026): a trend-aware transformer with 28 exogenous covariates on **daily EURUSD, USDJPY and GBPUSD**. It improves directional accuracy by 8.5–22.8% over the random walk and baselines. In **about one year** of backtests, cumulative returns were **18%, 25% and 18% before costs (Sharpe above 1.8)** and **7%, 19% and 9% after "conservative" costs and slippage**, while baselines turned negative. The authors' seminar page attaches Sharpe above 1.8 to the net figures, which **conflicts** with the abstract. This is a preprint with no replication — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12727v2); [University of Warsaw seminar page](https://www.wne.uw.edu.pl/en/research/conferences-and-seminars/upcoming-events/dinggao-liu-university-british-columbia-present-study-co-conducted-prof-robert-slepaczuk)
- **López-Herrera, González Maiz-Jiménez & Reyes-Santiago** (Discover Artificial Intelligence, Springer, 2025) test seven ML methods (logit, trees, RF, GBM, AdaBoost, XGBoost, NN) on **eight USD pairs, 2018–2023**, with time-series CV and dynamic institutional transaction costs. **Simpler models**, particularly logistic regression trained with a mean-absolute-directional loss, did best. Majors stayed profitable at round-trip costs below 0.4%, EM became unviable above 1.0%, and **most models became unprofitable at actual costs for a medium-cost pair**. The search snippet and a fragmentary copy were the only access — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44163-025-00424-4)
- Many 2024–2025 EURUSD deep-learning papers report **only accuracy or error metrics** (MAPE, R²), not trading P&L net of costs. Examples: a dual-input LSTM — [MDPI Mathematics 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7390/13/9/1472); a decomposition plus Bi-LSTM/GRU hybrid — [IJFS 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-7072/13/3/151); an MLP/RF/LSTM comparison (LSTM R² 0.92 on monthly or quarterly data) — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/applied-mathematics-and-statistics/articles/10.3389/fams.2025.1654093/full); LLM plus DL fusion — [arXiv 2408.13214](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.13214)
- A deep-RL FX trading paper that models transaction costs explicitly notes that trades must clear costs and that ML struggles with long-horizon prediction — [arXiv 2106.03035 (2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.03035)

### Inferences
- **The pattern across careful studies:**
  1. Linear and regularised models are at least as good as deep nets for FX.
  2. Deep nets help most as **trend-signal and position-sizing refiners on multi-asset futures**, not FX-only.
  3. Edges are small relative to costs: Lim et al. break down at about 2–3 bp, comparable to retail all-in costs on majors and below those on crosses.
  4. Headline DL "accuracy" papers are not evidence of tradability.
- **Overfitting risk is extreme.** FX has very low signal-to-noise. A transformer with 28 covariates evaluated on about one year of out-of-sample data (EXFormer) cannot distinguish skill from luck. For a sense of scale, a true Sharpe of 1 has a standard error of about 1 over one year.
- **If ML is ever tried:** restrict it to (i) a regularised logistic or linear model that combines the existing trend signals (1/3/12-month TSMOM plus breakout members) into one forecast, (ii) trained walk-forward with a turnover penalty and a cost-aware no-trade band, and (iii) judged only on a multi-year hold-out against the plain equal-weight ensemble. The **plain ensemble is the benchmark to beat**, and it should win by default.
- **Verdict:** **Do not build ML as a primary FX strategy.** At most, use it later as a research-only signal combiner, validated separately, with low expectations.

### Gaps
- No 2024–2026 peer-reviewed FX ML study with **multi-year out-of-sample, net-of-retail-spread** results was found.
- The Momentum Transformer's FX-specific and net-of-cost results are unknown (paywalled), and so is FRTZ's economic value in Sharpe terms.
- López-Herrera et al. cost assumptions and exact net returns are unverified (fragment only).

## 7. Realistic net Sharpe a small retail FX bot could expect in 2026, by family

### Takeaway
No source directly estimates **retail** net Sharpe by FX strategy family for 2026. Combining the evidence: an FX-only, daily-bar, 1–12 month, vol-scaled trend ensemble on G10 majors is the only family with a plausible positive expectation, of roughly **0.2–0.5 net**. Cross-sectional momentum, classic technical rules on majors, mean-reversion fades, session strategies and ML have **no cost-surviving retail evidence** and should be expected at about **0 or below** after costs. The strongest direct evidence on retail outcomes is negative: technical-indicator use is associated with *worse* individual FX trader returns.

### Cited Findings
- Multi-asset trend after 2/20 fees and costs, 1880–2016: about **0.4 Sharpe** (CXO's summary of Hurst–Ooi–Pedersen). A retail bot pays no 2/20, but it trades **one asset class** — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/momentum-investing/trend-following-with-intrinsic-momentum-over-the-very-long-run)
- FX trend sleeve, 1985–2012, gross: about **0.87** (AQR exhibit as surfaced by search) — [AQR PDF](https://www.aqr.com/-/media/AQR/Documents/Insights/Journal-Article/Demystifying-Managed-Futures.pdf)
- Walk-forward FX-only trend on seven majors, 2003–2025: **0.43** for the best blend, and only USDJPY (0.78) and EURUSD (0.54) clear 0.5 (cost model not visible in the extracts; non-peer-reviewed) — [QuantInsti](https://www.quantinsti.com/articles/trend-following-strategies-major-currency-markets-epat-project/)
- Short-term trend in small-tick markets (reportedly including FX) has not delivered reliable returns since about 2009 — [arXiv 2607.01550](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01550); [alphaxiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2607.01550)
- Net-of-fee SG Trend Index path: **+27.35% (2022)**, a **−20.4% drawdown (May 2024–May 2025)**, **+2.4% (2025)** and **+15.7% YTD (early October 2026)** — [Hedgeweek](https://www.hedgeweek.com/flat-december-sees-ctas-complete-record-year-2022/); [Cambridge Associates](https://www.cambridgeassociates.com/insight/does-trend-followings-recent-struggle-signal-that-the-strategy-is-structurally-broken/); [TTU Dec 2025](https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/trend-following-performance-report-december-2025/); [TTU Oct 2026](https://www.toptradersunplugged.com/trend-following-week-in-review-october-2-2026/)
- Cross-sectional currency momentum after 2010: no significant excess returns (weak thesis evidence). EM momentum after actual costs: about 1–3% a year, decaying to losses late in the sample — [Aalto](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/items/0db57fa9-e5c6-4b7d-9dd8-1ae15a8940c6); [Otago](https://ourarchive.otago.ac.nz/esploro/outputs/doctoral/Momentum-Trading-Strategies-in-Financial-Markets/9926480445101891)
- Technical rules on majors: profits disappeared by the early 1990s (NWU 2009). EM technical profits are "illusory" after data-snooping correction (KSW 2014) — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/fipfedlwp/2006-046.htm); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/p/bir/birmec/13-09.html)
- Actual individual FX traders, 2005–2009: technical-analysis use is negatively associated with performance — [Abbey & Doukas](https://www.efmaefm.org/0DOUKAS/publications/pdf/IsTechnicalAnalysisProfitable_12-14-2011.pdf)
- Intraday fix and time-of-day effects are described by their authors as exploitable by low-cost investors only — [Krohn, Mueller & Whelan draft](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0007/190753/Krohn_Mueller_Whelan_Jun2018_FXPremiaAroundTheClock.pdf)
- A London-breakout vendor backtest on GBPUSD, 2016–2026: −60% — [Backtrex](https://backtrex.com/en/backtests/london-breakout-gbp-usd)
- Deep-learning trend gains vanish above about 2–3 bp of costs (multi-asset futures) — [arXiv 1904.04912](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.04912)

### Inferences
- **Summary table (my judgement from the cited evidence; "net" means after retail spreads, commission and financing on majors):**

| Family | Codeable rule (default parameters) | Best published evidence (gross/net, period) | Recent regime | Retail cost sensitivity | Data-mining risk | Expected net Sharpe 2026 (my estimate) | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

- **Why the 0.2–0.5 range for trend.** The upper end comes from the QuantInsti out-of-sample result (0.43) and the historical FX sleeve (0.87 gross, in a favourable period), haircut for publication and selection decay. The lower end allows for the 2010s-style weak FX trend regimes and for retail financing costs on spot positions held for months. A 7-pair FX book is dominated by the USD factor and has few independent bets.
- **Statistical power:** FX has 50+ years of daily data for majors, far more than BTC. That makes the *existence* of FX trend well established. But the regime break around 1990 (technical rules) and around 2009 (short-term trend) means that **old data overstate today's edge**. Weight post-2008 results most.
- **What would change the verdict:** (i) a measured low or negative correlation between the FX trend book and the BTC trend sleeve, which would make even a 0.3 FX Sharpe valuable to the combined bot; (ii) the costs researcher's financing (swap) markups. If those are large, longer-holding trend loses a meaningful slice of return and the case weakens.

### Gaps
- **No study measures retail net Sharpe by FX strategy family**, and no 2026-specific estimate exists. The table above is my synthesis.
- The FX-sector attribution of the 2024–25 CTA drawdown and the 2026 recovery is unknown.
- No source covers the correlation of FX trend returns with BTC trend returns.
